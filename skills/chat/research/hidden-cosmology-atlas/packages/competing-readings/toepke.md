<h2>Did the BND take charge of Toepke’s network?</h2>
<p><strong>Finding.</strong> Thomas Wegener Friis reports that the Danes transferred the Holstein network to the BND in 1956. The archival citation for the offer is traceable. The individual who accepted operational control and the effective day remain unidentified.</p>
<h3>Two readings of the same record</h3>
<p><strong>Handover completed.</strong> Friis explicitly describes a completed transfer in a review drawing on declassified German records. He also says Lunding asked Gehlen to help Toepke join the Bundeswehr. Those are two actions involving different recipients. <a href="https://tidsskrift.dk/soenderjydskeaarboeger/article/download/119736/167503#page=8">Review, printed p. 209, right-hand page</a>.</p>
<p><strong>An offer with an uncertain operational outcome.</strong> Friis and Schmidt-Eenboom describe Lunding offering the network and seeking Heusinger’s help. Their notes cite a memorandum about a Hamburg conversation on 8 March 1956, written on 13 March. These dates identify the meeting and its record. The citation leaves open whether Lunding personally attended, which agents remained active, and who signed for them. Both accounts involve Friis. They cannot count as two independent witnesses to completion. <a href="https://tidsskrift.dk/frakrigogfred/article/download/163931/205347/364145">Study, notes 32 and 36–37</a>.</p>
<h3>What would each reading predict?</h3>
<p><strong>POSIWID.</strong> Look for what changed after March. If the network changed hands, surviving agents might receive new reporting instructions, payments or radio schedules. Toepke entering military service is compatible with either outcome.</p>
<p><strong>Hanlon’s alternative.</strong> Divided responsibilities or routine gaps in filing could leave an incomplete record even after a genuine transfer. A missing acceptance letter alone cannot decide between completion and a lapsed offer.</p>
